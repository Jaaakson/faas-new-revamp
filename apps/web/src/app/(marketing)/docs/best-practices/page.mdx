# Best practices

Most integration problems are not API problems. They are decisions about where
face authentication sits in your product, and they are much cheaper to get right
before launch than after.

## Treat it as a factor, not the factor

Fvas ships **heuristic** presentation-attack detection, not certified PAD. It
catches a face held up on a phone screen and a low-quality print. It does not
catch a good screen at the right angle, a high-quality print, a prepared video,
or a deepfake.

That makes it appropriate as a **step-up factor in a supervised or
semi-supervised context** — where the attacker also needs the account, the
device, and the session. It is **not** appropriate as the sole control for
unsupervised remote onboarding against a motivated adversary.

<Callout variant="warning" title="This is a scoping statement, not a disclaimer">
  If your threat model includes a determined remote attacker with a printer and a
  4K monitor, you need certified PAD, and you should not ship this as your only
  control. We would rather tell you now than have you find out in an incident.
</Callout>

## Handle three outcomes, not two

The single most common integration bug is treating `inconclusive` as
`rejected`.

```js
// Wrong. Tells someone with a dirty lens that they are an impostor.
if (result.decision !== "verified") denyAccess();

// Right.
switch (result.decision) {
  case "verified":    grantAccess(result.confidence); break;
  case "rejected":    denyAndLogPossibleFraud();      break;
  case "inconclusive": showGuidanceAndRetry(result.guidance); break;
}
```

Beyond the user-facing harm, collapsing the two poisons your own fraud metrics:
a spike in bad lighting will look exactly like a spike in attacks.

## Use the confidence band

A boolean throws away information your risk engine can use. Every `verified`
result carries a band relative to the threshold that produced it:

| Band | Margin above threshold | Suggested response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `high` | ≥ 0.15 | Proceed. |
| `medium` | 0.05 – 0.15 | Proceed for ordinary actions. |
| `low` | 0 – 0.05 | Verified, but marginal. Step up for anything valuable. |

A `low` band on a password change or a large transfer is exactly where a second
factor earns its cost.

## Enroll well, once

Verification accuracy is decided at enrollment. A profile built from three
careless frames will produce false rejections forever, and no threshold tuning
will fix it.

- **Three captures, mild angle variation.** Straight on, slightly left, slightly
  right. "Slightly" matters — past about 25° the pose gate rejects the frame.
- **Show the per-frame feedback.** Every response carries `images[].guidance`
  even on success. Use it; it is how someone learns their lighting is marginal
  before it becomes a support ticket.
- **Re-enroll after a significant appearance change.** New glasses are fine. A
  beard grown out over six months is worth a fresh profile.

## Do not tune the threshold blindly

The presets carry measured error rates from a real calibration run. A custom
threshold does not — we report nothing rather than a reassuring zero, because we
have not measured it.

| Preset | Threshold | FAR | FRR |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lenient | 0.32 | 1.0×10⁻⁵ | 2.73% |
| **Balanced** | **0.38** | **8.0×10⁻⁶** | **3.06%** |
| Strict | 0.45 | 8.0×10⁻⁶ * | 4.21% |

\* Identical to Balanced because 500,000 impostor pairs cannot resolve a lower
rate. Strict is defence in depth; its extra benefit is **unmeasured**, not zero.

If false rejections are hurting you, look at capture quality before the
threshold. Loosening the threshold trades security for a problem better fixed
with better lighting.

## Retry safely

Send an `Idempotency-Key` on every verification. Network timeouts are the normal
case at scale, and without one a retry costs you a second charge and produces a
second session row.

```js
headers: { "Idempotency-Key": crypto.randomUUID() }
```

The first response is replayed for 24 hours. Reuse the same key for the same
logical attempt; generate a new one for a genuinely new attempt.

## Scope your keys narrowly

A key that can only verify cannot delete your subjects. Create one key per
integration with only the scopes that integration needs, rather than one key
everywhere:

- The blast radius of a leak is what you scoped, not your whole account.
- `last_used_at` tells you which keys are actually live, so retiring an old one
  is not a guess.

Rotate on a schedule. **Rotate** keeps the old key alive for 24 hours so you can
redeploy; **revoke** takes effect on the next request and is what you want when
a key is known to be compromised.

## Store your own consent record

You are the data controller; we are the processor. Pass `consent_reference` when
you create a subject so the link between your consent record and our biometric
data is explicit rather than reconstructed later under audit pressure.

## Delete when you should

`DELETE /v1/subjects/{id}` physically destroys the templates — there is no soft
delete for biometric data. Wire it to your own account-deletion flow now rather
than discovering during a subject-access request that you have no way to honour
one.

## Verify webhook signatures

Every webhook is signed. Verify it, and use a constant-time comparison — a plain
`==` leaks how many bytes matched. Dedupe on `Fvas-Event-Id`: delivery is
at-least-once, and we say so plainly rather than implying a guarantee we cannot
make.

## Monitor the things that move first

- **Inconclusive rate.** Rising means your capture environment degraded — a new
  device, a new location, a changed camera.
- **p95 latency.** Ours, not yours; the timing breakdown in each response splits
  detect, embed, and match.
- **Quota burn.** We notify at 80% and 100%, but a sudden change in slope is
  worth knowing about earlier.
